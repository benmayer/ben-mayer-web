Urban Sports Club is a sports membership aggregator, during my time there were 300k paying members, 10k+ partner venues across Europe.

## My role 

I owned the end-user experience across app and web, B2C and B2B2C, first as Lead Prodcut Manager for growth and retention (2019-2022), then as Head of Product (2021-2023).

What taught me the most are the projects that didn't go perfectly. Here's what I learnt, and what I'd do differently today.

### All rebuilds take longer than planned

No shit, but seriously: here's why they're so hard to estimate, no matter how carefully you plan.

When I joined Urban Sports Club in 2019, iOS and Android had drifted into two different products on codebases nobody wanted to maintain. We planned the rebuild carefully. We broke the whole app down into features, then epics, and estimated those. As we started each epic we broke it into stories and estimated again. If the stories added up to more than the epic, we knew our original estimate had been off, which is to be expected, and we could either adjust scope or move the timeline.

The process is sound and I still use it. Its limitation shows up when you're rebuilding an existing product against a fixed deadline, because that's when the scope lever stops working. Every cut looks like a regression against the app people already have, so scope cuts rarely happen. Any unexpected delay, and there is always one, can't be absorbed anywhere except the timeline.

Now I go into any rebuild with an explicit answer to "what is the expectation". If there's a fixed deadline, I get agreement upfront on which gaps against the old product are acceptable, if any, and use Shape Up to deliver against it. Without that agreement the scope is "everything, but better", and the timeline has to stay flexible.

### First sport aggregator to launch live-streamed online classes


In March 2020 every venue we worked with was about to close. "No partners, no party" was a slogan the partner team used at the time, and it was starting to look real. We bet on live-streamed classes and started building before the closures were announced. We went live on 23rd March, the day the German lockdown started, with streams tied into subscriptions and check-in limits from day one, so partners kept earning and members could keep working out with their favourite studios.

What makes it memorable to me is what happened to our delivery. For months before, we'd been slow, and we'd spent retro after retro analysing our process with little to show for it. Then suddenly we were out-shipping better-funded competitors by weeks. What changed wasn't the process. It was that this was the only priority leadership cared about, everyone understood what was on the line, and everyone had skin in the game.

Two things stuck with me. The most important part of any strategy is the list of things it says no to. And a team with skin in the game will outperform a team trading time for money, every time.

### Sometimes better arguments doesn't beat more influence


After live-streaming worked, on-demand video looked like the obvious next step. I didn't think users would adopt it. I had run interviews the year before where one finding was that, given the choice, users preferred community over video on demand content.

I argued the case on user value and jobs to be done, and got nowhere. We built VoD without testing the hypothesis first and the adoption was tiny.

The lesson wasn't about the feature. I'd just been promoted to Head of Product and assumed the title meant my arguments would carry further than they did. They didn't. I was making a product case into a decision that wasn't being made on product grounds, and I never took the time to understand what the initiative meant to the person driving it.

Now I look one step deeper before I argue. When a decision doesn't seem to make sense, there's usually a motivation I can't see, or information sitting on one side of the table and not the other. So I start by understanding what the other person needs from the outcome, and I try to get agreement on how we'll decide before arguing about what to decide. Working with the stakeholder, understanding what drives them, has changed more outcomes for me than any amount of product framework has.

### Delighting users should never lose you money


Until 2022 the goal had been growth. Then the financing environment turned and profitability became the goal almost overnight. We were losing money: the bigger plans lost money per member, and a small group of heavy users generated most of the payouts, B2B2C was subsidising B2C.

So we had to make the product less generous and less delightful: charges for no-shows, caps on visits to the most expensive studios, tighter caps per day and per month. It worked. The unit economics improved and the company was later acquired by Wellhub. It also upset members, understandably. We had been selling something close to unlimited and were visibly making the product worse.

I'd been convinced there was a win-win-win in there somewhere, but the business model had always been the problem. We owned the subscriptions and paid partners per check-in, which meant our most engaged users cost us the most money. That's fundamentally flawed.

Today it's one of the first things I review when I build anything. If user value and margin pull against each other by design, you will eventually be asked to reduce user value. It was quite a unique model at the time. Today AI companies face the same thing with token-based pricing, and they've solved it the same way, with caps on usage. I'd expect them to face the same trade-off as soon as their financial goals change.
